A Perspective on “The ASA President’s Task Force Statement on Statistical Significance and Replicability”

Author

Christopher Tong

The ASA President’s Task Force on Statistical Significance and Replicability (Benjamini, et al., 2021), presents at least two ideas in their Statement that deserve close scrutiny.

First, their Statement conveys the impression that statistical summaries are tools to quantify uncertainty, tacitly conflating variation with uncertainty. For example, they write:

  • “P-values are valid statistical measures that provide convenient conventions for communicating the uncertainty inherent in quantitative results.”

  • “Capturing the uncertainty associated with statistical summaries is critical. Different measures of uncertainty can complement one another; no single measure serves all purposes. The sources of variation that the summaries address should be described in scientific articles and reports. Where possible, those sources of variation that have not been addressed should also be identified.”

  • “The theoretical basis of statistical science offers several general strategies for dealing with uncertainty. P-values, confidence intervals and prediction intervals are typically associated with the frequentist approach. Bayes factors, posterior probability distributions and credible intervals are commonly used in the Bayesian approach. These are some among many statistical methods useful for reflecting uncertainty.”

However, the statistical summaries listed above are measures of precision based on observed variability, with respect to a specified statistical model. They cannot capture uncertainty implied in the design and execution of the study, nor do they convey statistical model uncertainty.

Consider two identical data sets, analyzed using the same statistical model, resulting in the same statistical summaries (including p-values), but generated by two different study designs: a prospective, randomized, blinded, clinical trial; and a retrospective case-control design. Despite having identical p-values, the uncertainty of the case-control study analysis far exceeds that of the randomized trial, due to the greater possibility of confounding factors that compete with the intervention as putative causal explanations for the results. This is a wholly different phenomenon than “sources of variability”. No abundance of statistical summaries can fully and quantitatively capture the inherent uncertainty implied by such features of study design and execution.

Further, the metrics supplied by statistical inference only have validity when the statistical model has been pre-specified prior to data collection, a rare situation obtainable only in confirmatory research. In most other settings, the data must be used iteratively to guide statistical model building, model criticism, and model selection. This is a process that improves model fit to the current data, but to the potential detriment of generalizability to other data. This results in the Optimism Principle (Picard & Cook, 1984): biased point estimates, underestimated variance estimates, and overstated significance (Chatfield, 1995).

Benjamini et al. (2021) are right to point to the assessment of claims in new data as a more reliable test of “replicability”, but this implicitly acknowledges that the outputs of statistical inference cannot fully carry the burden of conveying uncertainty. In practice, several fields implement distinct “learning” and “confirming” stages of research, each using its own experiments and data. Examples include genome-wide association studies (Lin, 2020) and especially phased clinical trials (ICH, 1997); Mogil & Macleod (2017) have advocated for a similar setup in pre-clinical research. Such practices explore uncertainty empirically, reducing the reliance on statistical metrics from single sets of data.

Second, in their Statement Benjamini et al. (2021) write that “the use of P-values and significance testing, properly applied and interpreted, are important tools that should not be abandoned.” However it is possible to do without p-values in some known instances. Consider the case of human vaccine efficacy studies, specifically the FDA’s original 2020 Guidance for Industry on “Development and Licensure of Vaccines to Prevent COVID-19” [Note 1]. The efficacy portion of the guidance specified the agency’s expectations for vaccine efficacy: “a point estimate for a placebo-controlled efficacy trial of at least 50%, with a lower bound of the appropriately alpha-adjusted confidence interval around the primary efficacy endpoint estimate of > 30%.” No p-values were mentioned anywhere in this document. I know of five major pivotal efficacy studies for COVID-19 vaccines published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2020-2021, subsequent to the issuance of the FDA guidance: Pfizer/BioNTech, Moderna/NIAID, Janssen (Johnson & Johnson), AstraZeneca/Oxford, and Novavax. All five included estimates of vaccine efficacy (based on 1 – the incidence rate ratio, estimated using different statistical models, such as Poisson regression or the Cox proportional hazards model) with either 95% confidence or 95% credible intervals. Only two of them (Moderna and AstraZeneca) also included p-values in the main NEJM papers (I did not search the supplementary files). To emphasize my earlier point on confirmatory studies, pivotal vaccine efficacy trials occur at the end of vaccine development, after an extensive (and in this case accelerated) series of preclinical and clinical studies, and when comprehensive prespecification of the trial protocol (including statistical analysis plan) is expected.

This is not an isolated example in vaccine clinical trials. See for example the FDA guidance on seasonal flu vaccines, or the book of Halloran et al., (2010). In Sec. 6.2, Halloran et al. (2010) discuss seven real-world vaccine trials. In each case, interval estimates of vaccine efficacy are provided; p-values are also reported in only two of these seven. I conjecture that many investigators in the vaccine clinical trial arena have been comfortable with primarily reporting interval estimation, with p-values as optional at best, for a very long time [Note 2]. The ASA statements and special issue had absolutely no influence on such long-standing attitudes, because (in this particular arena of medical research) we were already living in “a world beyond \(p < 0.05\)”.

As Deming (1960) observed, “The statistician’s report to management should not talk about probabilities. It will merely give outside margins of error for the results of chief importance. Administrators are not interested in probabilities; they need to know what figure they can use and within what range they may depend on it.”

[Note 1] The original 2020 FDA guidance for COVID-19 vaccines may no longer be extant on the FDA website.

[Note 2] To confirm or refute my conjecture, I invite readers to examine the clinical efficacy sections (Sec. 14) of the package inserts for all licensed human vaccines in the U.S., and tabulate how many provide one or more of the following: interval estimates of vaccine efficacy, p-values, or other inferential statistics.
